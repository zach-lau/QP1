\section{Introduction}
State-space models are a widely applied and useful tool whenever we are
modelling the evolution of some sort of state over time given observations
which are dependent on the underlying state. Examples include modelling
the behaviour of animals given tracking data, the location
of a plane given radar readings, or the state of a climate system given
observations at set of weather stations.

The classical frequentist approach
to estimating a continuous state is the Kalman filter \cite{simon2006}. This
method starts with an initial estimate of the state, as well as a
quantification of the uncertainty of that estimate. The estimate and uncertainty
quantification are iteratively used to forecast the next step, and then
updated based on observations from that time step. However, the basic
Kalman filter only applies when our physical model is linear, and noise
is Gaussian. This makes it difficult to account for non-linear effects. For example,
we cannot account for pitch, yaw and roll in the vehicle dynamics model.
The extended Kalman filter addresses this problem by using non-linear dynamics
for the forecasting step, but using a linearized model to update the forecast
covariance. Extended Kalman filters work well for small dimensional problems.
However, they may suffer from two main problems. Firstly, the covariance
updates remain inexact, which may cause instability \cite{evensen1992}.
Furthermore, when the state space is high dimensional, it becomes extremely
costly to propogate and update a dense covariance matrix. This problem
inspired the ensemble Kalman filter \cite{evensen1994}\cite{evensen2003}.
The ensemble Kalman filter addresses these issues by maintaining a particle
approximation of the filtering distribution. Crucially, the number of particles
required for an accurate representation of the distribution is often much lower
than the dimension of the state space. This enables a low-rank representation
of the covariance matrix.

The ensemble Kalman filter is state of the art for high dimensional
climate based state space models. However, it is still constrained by the
Gaussian constraints on the observation noise and the modelling noise introduced
during the forecasting steps. This restraint restricts us, for example, from
modelling skewed or heavy-tailed distributions such as rainfall. In
low-dimensions, these constraints can be relaxed by Markov Chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC), Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) methods \cite{chopin2020} or Mixture Kalman
Filter \cite{chen2000}. These methods typically work by propogating an ensemble
of weighted particles through time. Unfortunately, in high dimensions these
techniques are known to suffer from weight degeneracy, wherein a single particle
is assigned all of the weight of the ensemble.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{GEnKF, PEnKF}
In \cite{katzfuss2020} the authors propose combining ensemble Kalman methods
with particle methods. In particular, the state is divided into two parts,
a high-dimensional part which is well modelled by Gaussian model dynamics,
and a low dimensional part which is not modelled well by Gaussian dynamics.
For example, the high-dimensional section may consist of a large spatial grid,
whereas the low dimensional part of the state space may consist of a set of
parameters over which we wish to perform Bayesian inference. Their key
algorithmic contribution is to use an ensemble Kalman filter to model the
high-dimensional portion of the state, but to do so within an SMC framework.
This reduces the effective dimensionality of the SMC problem, allowing one to
incorporate both high-dimensional latent spaces, as well as non-Gaussian
parameters -- a combination previously inaccessible to either of SMC methods or
ensemble Kalman filter methods on their own.

\subsubsection{Hierarchical State Space Models}
The generic hierarchical state space model considered in \cite{katzfuss2020} is
shown below.
\begin{align}
    \theta_t | \theta_{t-1} &\sim p(\theta_t | \theta_{t-1}) \label{eq:prior}\\
    \vx_t | \vx_{t-1}, \mathbf\theta_t &\sim \Norm_n(\mathcal M(\vx_{t-1}), \mathbf Q_t) \\
    \vy | \vx_t, \mathbf\theta_t &\sim \Norm_{m_t}(\mathbf H_t \vx_t, \mathbf R_t) \\
    \vz_t | \vy_t, \mathbf\theta_t &\sim f_t(\vz_t | \vy_t, \mathbf\theta_t) \label{eq:transform}
\end{align}
This model extends the model typically used in Kalman filtering probmes by
introducing a non-Gaussian parameter in line~\ref{eq:prior}, and a transformation
which can account for non-normality in the observations in line~\ref{eq:transform}.
Due to these extensions, a traditional ensemble Kalman filter is inappropriate.
However, when the dimension of $\vx$ is very high, such as a large spatial grid,
then SMC methods will struggle from weight degeneracy.

\begin{example}[Rainfall model]\label{ex:rainfall}
A practical example of such a hierarchial state space model is the rainfall
model provided in \cite{katzfuss2020} and based on 
\cite{sanso1999} and \cite{sigrist2012}. This model is shown below
\begin{align}
    \theta_t &\sim p(\theta_t) \\
    \vx_t | \vx_{t-1} &\sim \Norm_n(\mathcal M_t(\vx_{t-1}), \mathbf Q_t) \\
    \vy_t | \vx_t &\sim \Norm_{m_t}(\mathbf H_t \vx_t , \sigma^2_t \mathbf I_{m_t})  \\
    z_{t,l} | \vy_t ,\theta_t &= y_{t,l}^{\theta_t} \mathbf{1}_{\{y_{t,l} > 0\}}, \quad l = 1, \ldots, m_t 
\end{align}
This model reflects a series of spatially correlated series of stations. The
rainfall at each station is log-normally distributed, and truncated below by
$0$. Futhermore, the rainfall at all locations is modulated by a time-dependent
global parameter $\theta_t$. 
\end{example}

\subsubsection{PEnKF}
The most basic method proposed by \cite{katzfuss2020} is the
``particle ensemble Kalman filter'' (PEnKF). This algoritihm maintains a
particle representation of the filtering distribution of $\theta_t$, as well as 
one ensemble for each $\theta_t$. The algorthm is described as follows
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initialize a set of $M$ particles from prior $p(\theta_0)$ witih weights
    $w^i = \frac 1 M$ and for each $\theta_0^i$ an ensemble of $N$ particles
    $\vx_0^{ij} \sim \Norm_n(\vx_0|\mu_{0|0}, \Sigma_{0|0})$
    \item For each time step $1, \ldots, T$
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item For each particle $i = 1, \ldots, M$
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Sample from a proposal $q_t(\theta^i_t | \theta^i_{t-1})$
            \item Propogate the ensmble. That is compute the \emph{forecast}
            by applying the model to each ensemble member. I.e. compute
            $\tilde \vx^{i,j}_{t} := \mathcal M_t(\vx^{i,j}_{t-1}; \theta_t^i)$
            for $j = 1, \ldots, N$
            \item Update particle weights
            \begin{align}
                w^i_t \propto w^i_{t-1}\mathcal L_t^Z(\vz_t | \theta_t^i, \tilde\vx^i_t)
                p(\theta_t^i|\theta_{t-1})/q_t(\theta_t^i|\theta_{t-1}^i)
            \end{align}\label{step:likelihood}
            \item Draw a new ensemble $\vx^{ij}_t$ afresh from the conditional distribution
            $p(\vx^{ij}_t | \mathbf\theta_{1:t}, \vz_{1:t})$ \label{step:sample}
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}
    \item If desired, resample particles $\theta^i$ to rebalance weights.
\end{enumerate}
The major immediate challenges required to make such an algorithm work include
estimating the likelihood term in step~\ref{step:likelihood}, and sampling from
the conditional distribution in step~\ref{step:sample}. In particular,
\cite{katzfuss2020} suggest using a normal approximation to the forecast
distribution $p(\tilde\vx^i_t | \vz_{t-1})$ based on an estimate of the mean
and covariance of the ensemble. Practically, we are also limited by the number of
$\theta$ particles $N$ we can use, since each particle requires its own
ensemble of $M$ further particles.

\subsubsection{GEnKF}
Under appropriate conditions on our model, the requirement for an ensemble of
ensembles can be relaxed, and instead a single ensemble can be used which
covers both $\vx_t$ and $\theta_t$. The required conditions are forecast
independence, and tractability of full conditional distributions (FCD's).

\begin{condition}[Forecast independence]\label{cond:forecast}
An HSSM is said to exhibit forecast independence if
\begin{align}
    p(\tilde\vx_t, \mathbf\theta_t | \vz_{1:t-1}) &=
        p(\tilde\vx_t | \vz_{1:t-1})p(\mathbf\theta_t|\vz_{1:t-1})
\end{align}
\end{condition}

\begin{condition}[Full conditional distributions]\label{cond:conditional}
An HSSM satisfies the full conditional distribution condition if we can
tractably sample from $p(\vy_t|\vx_t,\theta_t,\vz_{1:t})$ and 
$p(\mathbf\theta_t | \vy_t, \vx_t, \vz_{1:t})$
\end{condition}

Condition~\ref{cond:forecast} is satisfied in relatively straightforward, but by
no means all-encompasing scenarios.  For example, it holds when when the
$\theta_t$ are independent, and the forecasting model $\mathcal M$ does not
depend on $\theta_t$, as in Example~\ref{ex:rainfall}.
Figure~\ref{fig:hssm-dag}, shows a partial causal graph for the hierarchical
state space model, and elucidates how these conditions imply forecast
independence.  Condition~\ref{cond:conditional} is more complicated to verify,
and also less clear cut as tractability to sampling depends on what inference
algorithm we are using. It most straightforward to show in the case of conjugate
priors, as happens in example~\ref{ex:rainfall}. The resulting algorithm is
described below
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Sample an ensemble $(\vx^i_0, \theta^i_0)$ for $i=1,\ldots M$
    from the prior at $t=0$
    \item For each time step $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Compute the forecast ensemble $\tilde \vx^i_t = \mathcal M(\vx^i_{t-1})$ for
        $i=1,\ldots, M$
        \item For each particle $i=1, \ldots, M$ perform Gibb's sampling
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Choose intial values for $\vy^i_t$ and $\theta^i_t$
            \item Until convergence
            \begin{enumerate}
                \item Sample $\vx^i_t$ by picking a random ancestor from $j=1$
                to $M$ and performing the EnKF update on
                $\tilde \vx^j_{t-1}$ given $\vy^i_t$ and $\theta^i_t$
                \label{step:enkf}
                \item Sample $\vy^i_t$ from $p(\vy^i_t | \theta^i_t, \vx^i_t, \vz_{1:t})$
                \item Sample $\theta^i_t$ from $p(\theta^i_t | \vy^i_t, \vx^i_t, \vz_{1:t})$
            \end{enumerate}
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
A key variance reduction technique suggested by \cite{katzfuss2020} is to run
each chain in parallel, and to use systematic resampling \cite{chopin2020}
across chains in step~\ref{step:enkf}. It is clear that
condition~\ref{cond:conditional} is necessary to make the Gibb's sampler work.
Forecast independence is required because the EnKF update relies on a
sample-based estimate of the forecast covariance of $\tilde x_t$. Under forecast
independence, the distribution of $\tilde x_t$ is independent of
$\mathbf\theta$, and therefore we can pool all elements of the ensemble to form
this estimate. Without forecast independence, the covariance would be dependent
on $\mathbf\theta_t$, and we would require a new ensemble for every
$\theta_t^i$, as in the PEnKF, or a reweighting of the existing ensemble
which is likely to suffer from weight degeneracy \cite{katzfuss2020}.

\subsection{Criticism of Existing Literature and Method}\label{sec:criticism}
The use of the EnKF within Bayesian state space-space models is certainly a
powerful idea which deserves further investigation. It successfully attacks
the central question of weight degeneracy in state-space models, although at
the cost of introducing bias. Unfortunately, this method as it currently stands
is extremely difficult to actually implement.

Firstly, as the authors in \cite{katzfuss2020} mention, but do not emphasize,
the GEnKF has only been shown to be reasonably implementable under fairly
restrictive circumstances. For example, if we work in the framework of
Conditions~\ref{cond:conditional} and \ref{cond:forecast}, we must abandon
very reasonable modelling choices such as using our parameter $\theta$ in
our actual model, or using the same $\theta$ over all time-steps. Furthermore,
the authors fail to discuss the difficulty of actually sampling from the
conditional distributions $p(\vy|\vx,\vz,\mathbf\theta)$ and
$p(\theta|\vx,\vy,\vz)$. Several problems may occur. Firstly, if $\vy$ is
moderately high dimensional and cannot be decomposed by dimensions, we still
have a nested high-dimensional sampling problem. Even if we have independence 
of $y_i$, and access to the conditinal density, we generally will still need
to perform MCMC to sample from this density. As this is done inside the Gibb's
sampler, this becomes a nested MCMC. We face exactly the same problem sampling
from $p(\theta|\vx,\vy,\vz)$. The requirements for nested MCMC significantly
slows down the sampling procedure, and introduces significantly more tuning
parameters such as step sizes and numbers of iterations, that must be chosen
wisely for effective inference. 

Furtheremore, the experimental setup presented in \cite{katzfuss2020} has
several peculiar implementation details, and fails to address key-scenarios of
interest. We first focus on their experiment on the rainfall model in
example~\ref{ex:rainfall}.  The authors perform this experiment for both a
fixed, known, $\theta$ and an unknown $\theta$. One strange choice they make
in this experiment is to infer $\theta$ using state-augmentation. This method
incorporates $\theta$ into the state vector. However, doing so violates the
model requiremenet that $\theta > 1$. The authors make no effort to reconcile
this conflict. The second peculiarity about this experiment is that the
authors only perform the filtering update for a single timestep. The intention
appears to be to isolate the effect of the ``update'' step in their algorithm.
Additionally, doing only a single update step allows them to develop a ground
truth for the posterior by running standard MCMC. However, in doing do so they
remove many of the important aspects of the state-space model, such as
information sharing between time points, or potential non-linear effects of
the dynamic model. The final peculiar choice made by the authors is to focus
their quantiative and qualitive results on the quantity $x 1_{x > 0}$. While
this is justified as being an estimate of the true rainfall, and in some sense
practical, it is perhaps more enlightening to look at inference of the latent
state $x$. In particular, since the observations are truncated below at $0$,
the most difficult coordinates to estimate, and a true test of the inference
method's capabilities is the estimation of the latent state at these ``dry''
locations.

In general, the authors also fail to run experiments in many key scenarios of
interest, or report all metrics of interest. Firstly we note that the GEnKF
is severly undertested. It is only tested in the rainfall model, which does 
not include a Gibb's step for $\theta$, is only single-step, and does not
test the filter under any model dynamics (let alone non-linear dynamics).
The PEnKF is somewhat more well tested by the cloud model, and is compared
with modified version of a standard particle filter. However, it is
never comopared directly to the GEnKF in any experiment. Furthermore, the
analysis of the PEnKF could be improved by evaluating the weight degenarcy
problem with quantitative tools such as the importance sampling ESS
\cite{chopin2020}.

\section{Simulation study on the rainfall model}
Here we present a simulation study to address some of the experimental
shortcomings of \cite{katzfuss2020} outlined in \ref{sec:criticism}. We aim to
address the following research questions which were inadequately addressed by
the current experiments.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can the GEnKF work with both non-Gaussian parameter $\theta$,
    and non-Gaussian observations, (i.e. with with a non-trivial parameter layer
    and transformation layer)?
    \item Does the PEnKF actually solve the weight degeneracy problem?
    \item Does condition~\ref{cond:forecast} lead to overly restrictive
    state-space models?
    % \item Will the proposed methods work for non-linear model dynamics?
    % model misspecification?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The GEnKF and PEnKF for the full rainfall model}
To answer the above questions we perform a simulation study on simulated
rainfall data. As much as possible, we use the same experimental setup
as \cite{katzfuss2020}. We make the following changes to the simulation
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We use a prior on $\theta$ of $\theta - 1 \sim \Gamma(20, 10)$,
    while \cite{katzfuss2020} use an unspecified normal prior.
    \item We use an AR1 model for the state space given by $\vx_t - \mu = 0.8(\vx_t - \mu) + 0.6(\mathbf u_t - \mu)$
    with the spatially constant mean $\forall i \; \mu_i = 0.2$, and
    $u$ is drawn from $p(\vx_0)$. \cite{katzfuss2020} do not specify
    a dynamic model.
    \item We run the model for $T=10$ timesteps, as opposed to the single
    update step in \cite{katzfuss2020}.
\end{enumerate}
Based on this specification we implement a particle filter, a
GEnKF and a PEnKF. The implementation of SIR uses the standard bootstrap
formulation with systematic resampling. For the GEnKF, we have a tractable
update for the conditional distribution $p(\vy | \vx, \mathbf\theta, \vz)$.
We use metropolis within Gibb's for the draw from $p(\mathbf\theta | \vx, \vy, \vz)$
(see Appendix~\ref{sec:genkf_mcmc} for details). To approximate the likelihood
for the PEnKF, we use a diagonal approximation to the empirical covariance
matrix. One may view this as an extreme form of tapering. However, its main
benefit is that it allows us to analytically marginalize over $\vx$ so that
quadrature is not required to compute the likelihood.
See Appendix~\ref{sec:penkf_likelihood} for implementation details.

The posterior mean and credible interval for $x$ at $T=10$ is shown for each
of the three methods in Figure~\ref{fig:filtering-bands} based on the weighted
particle approximation. While we don't have access to the ground truth
poseterior, the true latent state is shown for comparison. Initial results
are fairly positive for both GEnKF and PEnKF. Qualitiatively they match the
true latent state closely and 95\% credible intervals have reasonable coverage.
The PEnKF seems to have a bit more spatial variability. We conjecture that this
may be due to the lower number of $\theta$ particles used, which introduces
correlation between elements of the respective ensembles. Furthermore,
we expect some differences in the distribution of the PEnKF from the GEnKF
due to the approximation of the likelihood we have used.

Quantitaitively, GEnKF
and PEnKF both perform much better than the particle filter. The RMSPE measures
the accuracy of the posterior-mean as a point-wise estimate averaged over space.
The Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) gives an evaluation of the entire
forecast distribution. We have also averaged this over space. In both scores,
the GEnKF performs the best, followed by the PEnKF, and both are significantly
than the prior, which we use as a baseline.

The second question we wish to answer, was if the PEnKF empirically solves
the weight degeneracy problem. To evaluate the severity of weight degenarcy,
we use the importance sampling effective sample size (ESS) for 
normalized weights \cite{chopin2020} defined by
\begin{align}
    \text{ESS} = \frac{N}{\sum_i w_i^2}
\end{align}.
On the same dataset, we run the particle filter and PEnKF with no resampling,
in addition to our previous runs with resampling.
The resuls are clearly poor for the particle filter -- an ESS of 1 indicates
that practically all the weight is on a single sample. This agrees with what we
see in Figure~\ref{fig:filtering-bands}. The ESS for the PEnKF is not great, but
also still significantly better than the particle filter. With frequenet
resampling, the PEnKF is still able to capture the distribution of $\theta$
reasonably well, especially considering that $\theta$ is 1-dimensional in our
study. Fluctuations in the ESS for the PEnKF may be due to how unexpected
observations are at that time point. Very unexpected observations cause large
shifts in the distribution which are difficult to capture by reweighting. We
answer this research question with a weak affirmative.

\subsection{Particle filter likelihood}
For the particle filter we require the log-likelhood.
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L(\theta, \vx; \vz)
\end{align}
We are using a particle
filter on all our latent variables, so we use the conditional log-likelihood
marginalizing over $\vy$. We have a point mass at zero, so instead of consdiering
densities with respect to the lebesgue measure, we consider densities with
respect to the Lebesgue measure plus a dirac delta at zero. Letting
$m$ be the dimension of $y$, $\lambda$ the Lebesgue measure and $\delta_0$
the Delta measure at $0$, we are using the likelihood with respect to
\begin{align}
    \nu := (\lambda + \delta_0)^n
\end{align}
Given the model in 
Example~\ref{ex:rainfall}, the coordinates $y_i$ of $\vy$ are \iid conditional
on $\vx$. This simplifies the likelihood. Based on the Jacobian of the
transformation from $\vy$ go $\vz$, we can find the likelihood
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L(\mathbf\theta, \vx; \vz) &:= 
        \prod_i \mathcal L_i(\theta, x_i; z_i) \\
    \mathcal L_i(\theta, x_i; z_i) &:=
        \begin{cases}
            \Phi\left(-\frac{x_i}{\sigma_t}\right)\quad &\text{if } z = 0 \\
            \frac{1}{\theta\sigma_t}z_i^{\frac 1 \theta - 1}\Norm\left(\frac{z_i^{1/\theta}-x_i}{\sigma_t}; 0, 1\right)\quad &\text{else}
        \end{cases}
\end{align}
The $z > 0$ term agrees with that presented in \cite{katzfuss2020}. They
omit the $z=0$ term, because they presented the likelihood in the context of
the GEnKF, which does not use the log-likelihood for updates of $x$. However,
to use this for the particle filter, we must include this term.

\subsection{PEnKF likelihood}\label{sec:penkf_likelihood}
For the PEnKF we need the marginal likelhood
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L(\theta; \vz)
\end{align}
This is different from the particle filter which required the likelihood of the
joint state $(\theta, \vx)$. In general, \cite{katzfuss2020} suggest using
an approximation of hte likelihood basd on the EnKF. Their procedure is as
follows
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Propogate the ensemble to get $\tilde x_t^{ij} = \mathcal M(x_{t-1}^{ij};\mathbf\theta_i)$
    \item Estimate $\mu$ and $\tilde\Sigma$, the mean and covariance of the propogated
    ensemble
    \item Approximate the prior on $\vx_t$ by a normal with mean $\mu$ and
    variance $\widehat\Sigma = \tilde\Sigma + \mathbf Q$ 
    \item Calculate the integral 
    \begin{align}
        p(\vz_t | \theta_{1:t}, \vz_{1:t}) &=
            \int p(\vz_t | \vy_t, \mathbf \theta_t)\Norm(\vy_t;\mathbf H\mu,\mathbf H\widehat\Sigma\mathbf H^\top + \mathbf R)d\vy_t \label{eq:enkf_likelihood}
    \end{align}
\end{enumerate}
In our case, however the observation model is conditionally deterministic given
$\vy_t$ and $\mathbf\theta_t$. Due to the changing support, this would make
Equation~\ref{eq:enkf_likelihood} unwieldy. Furthermore, in the case of our
rainfall model, we have conditional independence of $z_i$ given $x_i$. This
allows us to simplify our likelhood with respect to the mixed measure from
before as \begin{align}
    p(\vz|\mathbf\theta_t,\vx_t) &= 
        \prod_i p(z_i | \theta, x_i)
\end{align}
Where 
\begin{align}
    p(z_i | \theta, x_i) &=
        \begin{cases}
            \Phi\left(-\frac{x_i}{\sigma_t}\right)\quad &\text{if } z = 0 \\
            \frac{1}{\theta\sigma_t}z_i^{\frac 1 \theta - 1}\Norm\left(\frac{z_i^{1/\theta}-x_i}{\sigma_t}; 0, 1\right)\quad &\text{else}
        \end{cases}
\end{align}
as before with respect to the mixed measure $(\lambda + \delta_0)^m$.
To find $p(\vz|\mathbf\theta)$ we marginalize over $\vx$
\begin{align}
    p(\vz|\mathbf\theta) &= \int p(\vz|\vx,\mathbf\theta)p(\vx|\mathbf\theta)dx
\end{align}
The conditional distribution $p(\vx|\theta)$ is approximated by adding noise to
the forecasting distribution to get $p(\vx|\theta) = \Norm(\mu,\widehat\Sigma + \mathbf
Q)$. Using the independence asumption
\begin{align}
    \int p(\vz | \vx,\theta)p(\vx|\theta) dx 
    &= \int \prod_i p(z_i | x_i, \theta)p(\vx|\theta) dx 
\end{align}

\subsection{A diagonal approximation}
We can analytically solve this integral in the case of diagonal approximation
to $\widehat\Sigma + \mathbf Q$.
In this we further simplify as 
\begin{align}
    \int \prod_i p(z_i | x_i, \theta)p(\vx|\theta) dx &= 
    \prod_i \int p(z_i |x_i,\theta)p(x_i|\theta)\,dx_i
\end{align}
In the case $z_i=0$ with $x_i \sim \Norm(\mu_i, \sigma^2_i)$ we have
$\frac{-x_i}{\sigma_t} \sim \mathcal N\left(-\frac{\mu_i}{\sigma_t},\frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_t^2}\right)$, and by 
Lemma~\ref{lemma:icdf}
\begin{align}
    \mathbb E\left[p(z_i=0| \theta, x_i)|\theta\right] &= \Phi\left(\frac{-\mu_i}{\sqrt{\sigma_t^2+\sigma_i^2}}\right)
\end{align}
In the case $z > 0$, we note that
\begin{align}
    \frac{z_i^{\frac 1 \theta} - x_i}{\sigma_t} 
        \sim \Norm\left(\frac{z_i^{\frac 1 \theta} - \mu_i}{\sigma_t}, \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_t^2}\right)
\end{align}
and so from Lemma~\ref{lemma:exponent}
\begin{align}
    \mathbb E p(z_i | \theta, x_i)
    &= \frac{1}{\theta\sigma_t}z_i^{\frac 1 \theta - 1}
        \E\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac 1 2 \left(\frac{z_i^{1/\theta}-x_i}{\sigma_t}\right)^2\right)\right] \\
    &= \frac{1}{\theta\sigma_t}z_i^{\frac 1 \theta - 1}
        \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\left(1+\frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_t^2}\right)}}
        \exp\left(-\frac{\left(z_i^{1/\theta}-\mu_i\right)^2}{2\left(\sigma_t^2+\sigma_i^2\right)}\right) \\
    &= \frac{1}{\theta}z_i^{\frac 1 \theta - 1}\Norm\left(z_i^{1/\theta};\mu_i,\sigma_t^2+\sigma_i^2\right)
\end{align}
We can also recognize this as the likelihood of $p(y_i|z_i,\theta)$ adjusted by the
Jacobian determinant $\frac{1}{\theta}z^{\frac{1}{\theta}-1}$.

The independence assumption implies only the diagional elements of the
covariance actually matter.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:icdf}
    If $X \sim \Norm(\mu, \sigma^2)$ then
    $\E\Phi(X) = \Phi\left(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}}\right)$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    From the definition of the CDF and tower rule
    \begin{align}
        \Phi(X) &= \P(Z \leq X | X) \quad Z \sim \Norm(0,1) \perp X \\
        \E\Phi(X) &= \P(Z \leq X) \\
        &= \P(Z - X \leq 0)
    \end{align}
    But since $X$ and $Z$ are both normal and $X \perp Z$
    \begin{align}
        Z - X &\sim \Norm\left(-\mu, 1+\sigma^2\right) \\
        \P(Z-X \leq 0) &= \Phi\left(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}}\right)
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:exponent}
    For $X \sim \Norm(\mu, \sigma^2)$
    \begin{align}
        \E\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{X^2}{2}\right)\right] &= 
            \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1+\sigma^2)}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mu^2}{2(1+\sigma^2)}\right)
    \end{align}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    We perform the integral by completing the square
    \begin{align}
        \E\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{X^2}{2}\right)\right] &= 
                \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\int \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac {1}{2\sigma^2}(x-\mu)^2\right) dx \\
        &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\int \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \left(x^2 - 2x\mu + \mu^2 \right)\right) dx \\
        &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\int \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left(x^2(1+\sigma^2) - 2x\mu + \mu^2 \right)\right) dx \\
        &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\int \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left(x^2(1+\sigma^2) - 2x\mu + \frac{\mu^2}{1+\sigma^2} -  \frac{\mu^2}{1+\sigma^2} + \mu^2 \right)\right) dx\\
        &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\int \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left((1+\sigma^2)\left(x - \frac{\mu}{1+\sigma^2}\right)^2 + \frac{\sigma^2\mu^2}{1+\sigma^2} \right)\right) dx\\
        &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\exp\left(-\frac 1 2\frac{\mu^2}{1+\sigma^2}\right)\int \exp\left(-\frac {1+\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2}\left(x - \frac{\mu}{1+\sigma^2}\right)^2\right) dx \\
        &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\exp\left(-\frac 1 2\frac{\mu^2}{1+\sigma^2}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\sigma^2}{1+\sigma^2}} \\
        &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1+\sigma^2)}}\exp\left(-\frac 1 2\frac{\mu^2}{1+\sigma^2}\right)
    \end{align}
    where the remaining integral is an unnormalized Gaussian integral, with $\int \exp\left(-\frac{a}{2}(x-m)^2\right)dx = \sqrt{2\pi/a}$ for $a = \frac{1+\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{GEnKF - Metropolis within Gibb's}\label{sec:genkf_mcmc}
To implement the GEnKF we need to sample from the rainfall model. This has
likelihood
\begin{align}
    p(\theta | \vx, \vy, \vz) \propto 
        p(\theta)p(\vx | \theta)p(\vy | \vx, \theta)p(\vz | \vy, \vx, \theta)
\end{align}
Under the causal structure in Figure~\ref{fig:hssm-dag}, this simplifies to
\begin{align}
    p(\theta)p(\vx)p(\vy | \vx)p(\vz | \vy, \vx, \theta)
\end{align}
For $z=0$, $p(\vz | \vy, \vx, \theta)$ does not depend on $\theta$ which
further simplifies the likelihood. We get the likelihood up to a constant is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L(\theta;\vx,\vy) := 
        p(\theta)\prod_{i;z_i > 0} \frac{1}{\theta}z_i^{\frac 1 \theta - 1}\Norm(z^{\frac 1 \theta};x_i,\sigma_t^2)
\end{align}
This agrees with what is presented in \cite{katzfuss2020}. The log-likelihood
up to a constant is then
\begin{align}
    \ell(\theta;\vx,\vy) := 
        \log p(\theta) + 
        \sum_{i:z_i > 0}\left[-\log \theta +\left(\frac 1 \theta - 1\right)\log z_i -\frac 1 {2\sigma_t^2}\left(z_i^{\frac 1 \theta}-x_i\right)^2\right]
\end{align}
with gradient
\begin{align}
    \nabla_\theta \ell(\theta;\vx,\vy) =
        \nabla_\theta \log p(\theta) +
        \sum_{i:z_i > 0}\left[-\frac 1 \theta - \frac{\log z_i}{\theta^2}
            + \frac{\log z_i}{\sigma_t^2\theta^2}z_i^{\frac 1 \theta}\left(z_i^{\frac 1 \theta}-x_i\right)\right]
\end{align}
In the rainfall model $\theta - 1 \sim \text{Gamma}(20, 10)$ (shape and rate),
so for $\theta > 1$
\begin{align}
    \log p(\theta) &= 20\log 10 - \log\Gamma(20) + 19\log(\theta - 1) - 10(\theta - 1) \\
    \nabla_\theta \log p(\theta) &= \frac{19}{\theta - 1} - 10
\end{align}
